A tide table looks like a list of numbers, yet each number comes from the same plain idea: the moon pulls on the ocean, and the ocean, being fluid, bulges toward it. Coastlines then ride through that bulge twice a day as the Earth turns underneath.

The timing is rarely tidy. A bay with a narrow mouth fills slowly and drains slowly, so its high water can lag the moon by hours, and a wide open beach can follow the moon almost exactly because nothing holds the water back.

Sailors learned these local habits the hard way. A harbor pilot would keep a notebook of when the water turned at each landmark, and over many seasons the notebook became more reliable than any general rule, because it captured the shape of that one coast.

Modern tables do the same thing with arithmetic. They add together a few dozen smooth waves, one for the moon, one for the sun, one for the tilt of the orbit, and so on, each tuned to a particular port from years of measured water levels, and the sum predicts the tide to within minutes.

That is why the table can be trusted on a calm day. It is also why a storm can break it, since wind pushes water in ways no wave of the moon accounts for.
