When a baker mixes flour and water, nothing much seems to happen at first, because the two simply sit together as a shaggy and unremarkable lump. Kneading changes that, since pushing and folding the dough lines up the proteins into long elastic strands that can hold a shape.

Those strands matter because yeast is busy underneath them. Yeast eats the sugars in the flour and gives off carbon dioxide, and the gas has nowhere to go except into tiny pockets that the elastic network stretches around, so the whole mass slowly puffs upward over an hour or two, doubling in volume while the baker does something else entirely.

Then the oven arrives. Heat makes the trapped gas expand even faster for the first few minutes, which is why a loaf springs upward right after it goes in. Soon the outer layer dries and browns, and the structure stops moving.

What you slice into later is a record of that sequence. Large irregular holes tell you the dough was wet and gently handled, while a tight even crumb suggests it was kneaded hard and baked in a tin, and both are good bread, only different answers to the same question about how much gas to keep.
