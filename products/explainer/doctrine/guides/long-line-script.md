## Long lines for the ear

This will be read aloud by one narrator while pictures play. The listener hears each sentence once, at the narrator's pace, and cannot look back. That changes what a good sentence is, though not by making it short.

An explanation moves in steps of reasoning, and a sentence is the natural container for one whole step. When one thing causes another, the step has three parts: what does the causing, what it acts on, and what comes of it. A person who understands the subject says those together, because for them it is one thought, and the words that join the parts (because, so, which means, until, unless) are where the listener hears the reasoning. Split into three flat statements, the same step loses those joins. The listener receives three facts that all sound equally important and has to work out which one led to which, while the narrator has already gone on to the next thing. So let a sentence run for as long as its step of reasoning runs, and keep a condition, a reason or a limit in the same sentence as the claim it belongs to. In a good spoken explanation a large share of the sentences are long for exactly this reason, and they are easier to follow than the same content cut small, because the listener is told how the facts connect.

Short sentences belong where the thought is short: a plain fact, a result the listener has been waiting for, a question, a change of direction. The script will have a good number of them. They are there because those thoughts really are small, and they are not a way of adding weight to a point.

What lets a listener follow a long sentence is the order of its parts. Say early what the sentence is about and what that thing does, and then let each clause add to the one before it, so that wherever the narrator takes a breath, everything said so far already makes sense. Built that way, a sentence never leaves the listener holding an unfinished structure. A writer who sets out to write longer sentences usually reaches for two shapes that do leave them holding one. The first is the long run-up: one or more clauses beginning with words like "because", "while" or "although" that have to be carried before the listener learns what the sentence is about. A lead-in of a few words is fine, and anything longer goes after the main statement. The second is the interruption: an aside, a definition or a qualification placed between a subject and its verb, or put in brackets, which a voice cannot carry. A detail worth saying gets a clause of its own after the thing it describes, or a sentence of its own. And length is never the aim. Two thoughts joined by a semicolon, or a string of facts joined by "and" with no reasoning between them, are separate sentences and are written as separate sentences. Nor is any of this a reason for the script to grow. A step of reasoning said in one sentence takes fewer words than the same step said in three, because nothing has to be repeated to tie the pieces back together, so the script covers the same ground in the same space.

Three illustrations follow, on subjects that have nothing to do with yours. Each holds one step of reasoning in one sentence, with the main statement near the front and every clause adding to the one before. They differ in shape on purpose, and none of them is a pattern to copy.

A kettle switches itself off because the steam from the boiling water is channelled down onto a small disc made of two metals bonded together, and since one of the metals expands more than the other as it heats, the disc snaps into a curve and pushes the switch open.

A guitar string pressed against a fret sounds higher because only the part between the fret and the bridge is free to vibrate, and a shorter string vibrates faster.

If you water tomato plants a little every day, their roots stay near the surface, where the water is, and the first hot week dries them out, whereas a plant that is soaked once a week has had to send its roots down after the water and comes through the heat.

When two things have just been mentioned, say the noun again where "it" could mean either one; on a page the eye sorts that out, and in the ear nothing does. Write numbers and symbols the way the narrator should say them.
